Workplace discrimination timing should be checked early.

Workplace discrimination concerns can involve internal HR steps, Pennsylvania Human Relations Commission filings, federal Equal Employment Opportunity Commission charges, and civil claims. The timing can depend on what happened, when it happened, who the employer is, what category is involved and whether any agency paperwork already exists. This page is a starting point, not legal advice for a specific situation.

Deadline awareness

Agency filing windows may be measured in months, not years.

Employment discrimination and retaliation concerns can involve HR records, PHRC filings, EEOC charges, union or grievance procedures, public-employment rules, accommodation requests, termination dates, discipline, harassment, pay issues or other workplace events. The correct timing may depend on the employer, protected category, agency, event date and procedural history.

Do not assume that an internal HR complaint, union process, employer investigation, mediation or informal discussion pauses an outside agency or court deadline. Preserve the record and ask for legal review promptly.

A helpful preparation step is to build a dated timeline. Include the workplace event, report to management or HR, employer response, discipline or termination, accommodation request and any agency filing or notice.

Do not assume HR pauses outside deadlines.

Internal HR processes generally do not pause external agency or court deadlines. If an outside deadline might be running, confirm the timing directly with an attorney rather than assuming the internal process protects the outside window.

Timeline to prepare

  • Date of the workplace event or conduct.
  • Date reported to HR, management, a supervisor, union, or agency.
  • Date of any employer response.
  • Date of discipline, termination, denial, schedule change, demotion, pay change, or other adverse action.
  • Date any agency paperwork was filed or received.

What to preserve

Organize the proof before details fade.

A clear record helps sort whether the concern is an internal workplace issue, an agency filing issue, a civil claim or something that needs more factual development.

Written communications

Save emails, text messages, HR portal messages, complaint forms, accommodation requests, discipline, evaluations, schedules, pay records, termination letters, policies, and employer responses with visible dates. Keep lawful copies outside employer systems where permitted. Do not delete the originals after copying.

Employment records

Keep offer letters, job descriptions, schedules, pay records, handbook pages, discipline records, evaluations, attendance records and termination papers. Identify which records came from the employer and when they were received.

Retaliation and discrimination timeline

A useful timeline should separate the protected concern from the employer action. Include dates for the workplace event, report to management or HR, employer response, adverse action, witness involvement, agency filings, letters, portal messages, and any right-to-sue communication.

Accommodation and leave records

If the issue involves a disability or religious accommodation request, parental or medical leave, or pregnancy-related accommodation, save the request itself, the employer's response, any supporting documentation referenced and any policy excerpts the employer cited.

Witness and HR records

List people with first-hand knowledge and describe what each person saw, heard, received, sent, or decided. Identify whether each person is a supervisor, HR employee, coworker, former employee, union representative, customer, vendor, or third party. Do not contact witnesses in a way that could create workplace issues or be characterized as pressure.

Agency paperwork and envelopes

If PHRC or EEOC paperwork has already been filed or received, save the charge, notice, response dates, right-to-sue letters, mailed envelopes, and portal messages. The mailing date or portal date may affect the next deadline.

Before contacting the office

Before you contact the office

Before contacting the office, avoid deleting records, changing originals, posting sensitive details publicly, contacting witnesses in a way that could create workplace issues, or assuming that an internal process protects an outside filing date.

Keep the first call or email focused on basics: employer name, work location, event dates, agency papers, timing notices and the type of workplace issue. Keep confidential details out of email until the firm completes a conflict check. Contacting the firm does not create an attorney-client relationship, and representation begins only after conflict review and an engagement agreement.

Deadline FAQ

Common employment timing questions.

Does an internal HR complaint protect my outside filing date?

Not necessarily. Internal procedures and external agency filing dates are generally separate. Confirm timing with an attorney rather than assuming one process pauses the other.

What if more than one event happened?

List every event with its date. Some timing rules may run from specific acts, and a lawyer generally needs the full timeline to evaluate which dates may control.

Should I keep personal notes?

A dated timeline can help you remember events, but preserve original records as well. Personal notes are generally not a substitute for emails, texts, notices, policies, evaluations, pay records, or agency documents.

What if I already received a right-to-sue letter?

A right-to-sue letter may create its own filing date. The letter itself should be reviewed promptly, along with the envelope or portal notice if available.

Does this page cover wage, contract, non-compete, or trade-secret claims?

No. Wage payment, overtime, contract, non-compete, and trade-secret disputes can follow their own rules and limitation periods. If those records are part of the same workplace situation, bring them too.
