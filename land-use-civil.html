Land use and civil litigation

Help when a property, business or civil dispute needs direction.

A zoning notice, lease problem, contract dispute, property question, business disagreement or civil claim can become urgent once a court, board, agency or response date is involved. An early look helps identify where the dispute belongs, what proof exists and whether a letter, negotiation, filing or court response makes sense.

Common issues

  • Landlord-tenant and lease disputes
  • Zoning, land use, and property issues
  • Property line, easement, access, and boundary disputes
  • Contracts and business disputes
  • Civil litigation in magisterial district court or the Court of Common Pleas

Civil disputes

Start with what happened and what shows it.

Civil, property and land-use disputes usually start with a written record. Keep the notice, contract, lease, deed, easement, permit, board paper, inspection report, estimate, invoice or dated message that shows what happened.

The first question is where the dispute belongs: court, a local board, a municipal office, an agency or pre-suit negotiation. Call sooner if a complaint, summons, board decision, lease notice, municipal notice, demand letter or appeal instruction has arrived.

Helpful records

  • Contracts, leases, deeds, easements, permits, and board notices.
  • Photographs, surveys, plot plans, maps, inspection reports, and repair estimates.
  • Emails, letters, invoices, payment records, delivery records, and dated text messages.
  • The complaint, summons, hearing notice, response date, denial, demand letter, or appeal instruction that started the timeline.
  • Any related insurance policies, indemnity provisions, guarantor information, escrow records, or prior agreements.

Keep the proof.

Do not rely on memory alone. Keep dated copies of key records, photographs, notices and communications.

Places the issue may land

  • Magisterial district courts
  • Court of Common Pleas
  • Zoning hearing boards
  • Administrative or municipal proceedings

Property and easement disputes

Property disputes often turn on records that show ownership, boundaries, use, notice, payment, damage, repairs or access. Useful records may include deeds, leases, surveys, photographs, inspection reports, correspondence, estimates, municipal notices, and prior agreements.

Zoning and land-use issues

Zoning and land-use questions often depend on timing and the municipal record. Bring the application, denial, notice of violation, permit papers, hearing notice, municipal correspondence, plans, maps, photographs, surveys, and any prior approvals. The first discussion should identify the property, municipality, board or office involved, notice date, hearing date and any response or appeal instruction.

Contracts and business disputes

Contract and business disputes often require a close look at the written agreement and the conduct that followed. Bring signed contracts, proposals, invoices, change orders, payment records, emails, text messages, delivery records, demand letters and any lawsuit papers.

Lease and landlord-tenant issues

Lease disputes can involve rent, deposits, repairs, possession, notices, property condition, lease terms, business use, or residential issues. Bring the lease, renewal papers, notices, payment records, photographs, inspection reports, repair requests, messages, and any court papers.

General civil litigation

Civil claims generally require careful evaluation of liability, damages, collectability, cost, timing, available proof, potential counterclaims and non-litigation options. A good timeline should connect the agreement or event, performance, alleged breach or harm, notice, demand, response and current response date.

Civil-dispute packet

Build a simple dispute packet

A helpful packet usually starts with:

  • the record that created the relationship, such as a contract, lease, deed, proposal, easement, or permit;
  • the record that triggered the dispute, such as a notice, denial, letter, demand, invoice, citation, complaint, or board decision;
  • proof of performance, payment, damage, repair, delivery, access, or communication;
  • photographs, maps, estimates, reports, and dated messages;
  • the next hearing date, response date, appeal instruction or other date listed on the papers.

Do not rely on memory alone. Keep originals separate, and organize copies by date.

Timing and notices

When to call soon

Some civil issues can be evaluated calmly over time. Others have response windows printed on the papers themselves. Calling promptly is generally appropriate when:

  • a complaint, summons, or lawsuit paper has been served and a response date is approaching;
  • a board, agency, or municipal office has issued a written decision and an appeal window may be running;
  • a lease notice, eviction notice, demand letter, contract notice or municipal notice lists a response date;
  • property is at risk from a pending sale, lien, sheriff's sale, recording, access dispute, or repair issue;
  • a document says you must appear, answer, appeal, respond, or file by a certain date.

If you are not sure whether the timing is urgent, have the papers in front of you when calling instead of relying on memory.

Have a notice or response date?

Call the office with the paper in front of you and identify the court, board or office, the date and the type of notice. Keep confidential details out of email until the office can complete a conflict check.
